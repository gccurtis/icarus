# Design - Marketing Surface Aesthetic

> Source mirror. Preserve this page as planning evidence; use the agent-facing execution packet when one exists.

<callout icon="✦" color="blue_bg">
	**Reviewed and retained — specialized marketing-surface authority.** This page adapts the governing Yesod design system to public surfaces without redefining it.
</callout>
> **Scope:** Visual style and aesthetics only. This page does not define application architecture, routes, components, CSS, implementation tokens, or interaction contracts.
# Purpose
Translate Taurus's visual doctrine into a public marketing surface that feels unmistakably related to the product while remaining more spacious and editorial than the working application.
The public site is the threshold to Taurus. It should communicate the same emotional promise as the workbench: a quiet workspace with infinite volume that centers the work, makes room for creativity and judgment, and lets information move without visual strain.
# Governing feeling
The desired experience is:
- **quiet** — little visual noise, interruption, or competitive emphasis;
- **spacious** — generous breathing room and a sense of volume beyond the immediate viewport;
- **centered** — the product and the user's work remain the visual subject;
- **soft** — edges, contrast, and motion do not feel brittle or aggressive;
- **light** — neither visually heavy nor cognitively dense;
- **fluid** — the story moves like a continuous current rather than a stack of unrelated panels;
- **precise** — restraint should feel intentional, not unfinished; and
- **inspiring** — the environment suggests possibility without becoming theatrical.
The site should reduce the felt stress of work. It should never resemble a crowded enterprise dashboard, a neon AI spectacle, or a generic productivity landing-page template.
# Relationship to the application
The application is an active workbench. The public site is a clearer, more spacious view into that workbench.
Preserve:
- the same emotional temperature;
- the same core color relationships;
- the same typographic character;
- the same sense of calm depth;
- the same accessible focus and legibility standards; and
- product-native visual evidence.
Adapt:
- use more negative space;
- create a larger editorial rhythm;
- show fewer simultaneous controls;
- let one real product artifact carry the visual argument; and
- keep supporting decoration subordinate to the work.
# Composition
## One center of gravity
Each viewport should have one clear visual subject. In the hero, that subject is the finished market-study work product and the concise statement beside it. In later sections, it may be a context change, provenance detail, or reusable methodology asset.
Avoid competing cards, multiple equal calls to action, dense navigation, and decorative illustration that pulls attention away from the product.
## Depth without heaviness
Use layering to imply a workspace with volume:
- a quiet base plane;
- a softly lifted product surface;
- restrained borders or shadows that establish separation; and
- occasional deeper space around the artifact.
Depth should feel atmospheric and architectural, not glossy or skeuomorphic. The page should remain readable if all motion is removed.
## Rhythm
Move between broad fields of space and concentrated moments of product detail. Long passages should be broken by meaningful hierarchy, not ornamental cards. Repetition should feel measured and musical rather than mechanically gridded.
# Color direction
Color should create calm, hierarchy, and recognition before it creates excitement.
Use:
- a warm, low-glare light foundation rather than stark white;
- deep neutral text with enough contrast for sustained reading;
- subdued borders and separators;
- one primary accent family for action and focus; and
- secondary semantic colors only where the product artifact needs to show provenance, context, state, or meaning.
Avoid:
- saturated gradients behind body text;
- large fields of bright brand color;
- several accent families competing in one viewport;
- neon cyan or violet used as generic “AI” decoration;
- low-contrast gray-on-gray minimalism; and
- color as the only carrier of meaning.
The current palette and resource-signature values are hypotheses governed by [Design — Visual Language, Tokens & Resource Signatures](https://app.notion.com/p/39ab6410e50281798739fa3a9e8931ac). Validate them against the intended feeling and accessibility requirements before treating them as permanent brand constants.
# Typography direction
Typography should feel composed, literate, and easy to inhabit.
Use:
- IBM Plex Sans for Taurus-authored display, navigation, and reading;
- IBM Plex Mono only for authentic machine-adjacent evidence;
- clear scale changes rather than many weights or styles;
- sentence case;
- generous line height and readable measure;
- concise labels; and
Avoid:
- all-caps navigation or section headings;
- oversized slogans that overwhelm the product;
- compressed body copy;
- decorative display type that competes with the work; and
- monospace as a generic signal of technical sophistication.
# Product imagery
The product itself is the primary visual asset.
The signature image should begin with a polished market-study deck or document at rest. Supporting frames may show:
- a changed industry, period, or geography;
- relevant narrative content updating;
- exact provenance attached to a consequential claim;
- an editable analytical instruction;
- a reusable slide or structure resolving against project context; and
- the method being saved for future use.
The image should feel like real knowledge work, not a fabricated dashboard. Use believable text density, professional editorial structure, and restrained annotation.
Do not lead visually with:
- a template gallery;
- a blank prompt box;
- a chatbot conversation;
- a spreadsheet chart changing;
- a generic network diagram;
- a humanoid AI image; or
- an abstract glowing orb.
# Motion language
Motion should express flow, continuity, and causality.
Good uses:
- a context value changes and relevant work settles into its new state;
- a provenance layer opens with a gentle spatial relationship to the claim;
- a reusable slide enters the deck without a theatrical transition;
- content shifts with continuity rather than flashing or resetting; and
- subtle entrance motion clarifies reading order.
Avoid:
- constant ambient movement;
- particles, cursor followers, parallax dependency, or scroll-jacking;
- rapid pulses associated with urgency;
- spinner-heavy “AI thinking” theatrics;
- motion that makes the page feel busy; and
- any essential explanation that disappears when reduced motion is enabled.
The current motion doctrine lives in [Design — Motion & Feedback Language](https://app.notion.com/p/39ab6410e50281dabbf3dc3f5b07adb0).
# Accessibility and legibility
The aesthetic fails if it is tiring or exclusionary.
Requirements:
- text and controls remain legible across common vision conditions and display environments;
- contrast is tested rather than assumed from the softness of the palette;
- focus is visually clear and harmonious with the page;
- reduced-motion presentation preserves the full story;
- product imagery has a meaningful text alternative or adjacent explanation;
- typography remains readable at zoom; and
- hierarchy does not depend only on color, position, or animation.
Use [Design — Accessibility & Visual Legibility](https://app.notion.com/p/392b6410e50281de8f06c206383e8d2f) as the governing accessibility reference.
# Content-density rules
- One central claim per section.
- One dominant artifact per viewport.
- No wall of equal product features.
- No more visual chrome than is needed to understand the work.
- Let the value proposition become more specific as the visitor moves down the page.
- Keep trust and security language visually sober.
- Let institutional IP feel like a realization, not a shouted slogan.
# Prohibited outcomes
The public site should never feel:
- cluttered;
- anxious;
- heavy;
- loud;
- brittle;
- over-engineered;
- cyberpunk;
- surveillance-oriented;
- like enterprise middleware;
- like a presentation generator; or
- like a generic AI startup assembled from familiar landing-page motifs.
# Visual review questions
Before approving a direction, ask:
1. Does the page feel like entering a quiet workspace with room to think?
2. Is the user's work, rather than the interface or AI, visually centered?
3. Can the core story be understood without animation?
4. Does the artifact look like a finished professional deliverable?
5. Is there one clear center of gravity in each viewport?
6. Do color, typography, depth, and motion all feel soft, light, and precise?
7. Is the page legible under zoom, reduced motion, and low-vision conditions?
8. Would removing decorative effects make the page stronger rather than empty?
9. Does the visual sequence support finished work first and the methodology library second?
10. Does the page feel inspiring without creating urgency or spectacle?
